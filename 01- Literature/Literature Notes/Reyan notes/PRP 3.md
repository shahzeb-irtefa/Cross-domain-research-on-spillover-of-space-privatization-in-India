**India's Space Economy, 2011–12 to 2020–21: Its Size and Structure**

_Reading notes — Primary Paper 3_

**Authors:** Sunil Mani (CDS Trivandrum), V.K. Dadhwal (NIAS Bengaluru), C.S. Shaijumon (IIST Trivandrum)

**Published in:** Space Policy (Elsevier), 2022 — DOI 10.1016/j.spacepol.2022.101524

**JEL codes:** L86, O31, O33, O34, O38

**Read alongside:** Indian Space Policy 2023 (separate note)

**Tags:** #primary-paper #space-economy #india #measurement

_One-line summary: this is the first serious attempt to put a rupee number on India's space economy. It lands at roughly USD 4–5 billion, about 0.2% of GDP, and it is dominated by DTH television rather than rockets or satellites. Basically the "before privatisation" picture._

# 1. Main Objective / Research Questions

The authors are pretty upfront that India has had a space programme for over fifty years, yet nobody has actually measured how big the space economy is or what it's made of. So the paper sets out to fill that gap.

The questions they are really asking are:

•     How big is India's space economy, in rupees, in dollars and as a share of GDP?

•     What is it made of — how much is manufacturing, how much is operations, how much is applications?

•     What role has the state (ISRO and the central budget) played in shaping it?

•     How productive has public money spent on space actually been, measured through a capital–output style ratio?

There's also a softer, background question running through the whole paper: the sector is moving from state-led to more market-led, so what does the starting point of that transition look like?

# 2. Research Design

## On the basis of enquiry

**Descriptive and exploratory, largely quantitative.** There is no hypothesis being tested. The paper maps out something that hadn't been mapped before, using numbers wherever they exist and informed estimates where they don't. It also leans a little on qualitative policy description (Box 1, Table 8) to explain why the numbers look the way they do.

## On the basis of objective

**Mainly descriptive, with a small analytical layer.** Most of the paper describes size, structure and trends. The productivity section (Section 5.3) tries to go one step further by relating outputs to inputs, but the authors themselves call it a crude indicator and refuse to draw firm conclusions from it.

## On the basis of applicability

**Applied research.** It is written to feed policy. The authors say directly that a clearer picture of the space economy should lead to more evidence-based space policy. It's not trying to build theory.

Framework-wise, they call their approach "eclectic":

•     **OECD framework** (inputs → activities/outputs → impacts) is used to describe the contours of the sector.

•     **UK Space Agency framework** (manufacturing, operations, applications, ancillary services) is used to actually measure size and structure, because it handles double counting better.

•     They also review the **US BEA satellite-account approach** but say it's hard to copy since detailed supply–use tables aren't easy for independent researchers to get.

Time period: the size estimates cover 2011–12 to 2020–21 (ten years), chosen because that's where reasonably good data exists for all three segments. Some tables go back to 1990 or 2000.

# 4. Analytical / Statistical Method

There's no econometrics here at all — no regression, no causal identification. It's accounting and descriptive statistics, done carefully. The main tools are:

•     **Growth rates and shares** — year-on-year growth, percentage shares of each segment, share of GDP.

•     **Deflating** — nominal budget converted to real terms using the implicit GDP deflator (2004–05 base up to 2010–11, then 2011–12 base).

•     **Ratio indicators** — space budget ÷ GDP (budget intensity), space assets ÷ net capital stock.

•     **Unit-cost × quantity estimation** for manufacturing: count of satellites and launchers built each year multiplied by average costs (about Rs 330 crore per satellite and Rs 114 crore per launcher).

•     **Proxy assumption** for satellite navigation/location services: taken as 5% of mobile telephony revenue reported by TRAI.

•     **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index** to measure concentration in the DTH market (comes out at about 0.28 on average for 2016–17 to 2019–20, i.e. highly concentrated).

•     **Unadjusted vs adjusted size** — the adjusted figure nets out the portion of the space budget that also appears inside "space operations", to avoid counting the same money as both input and output.

•     **Capital–output ratios** for productivity:

–    Estimate 1 = space budget ÷ size of space economy

–    Estimate 2 = space budget ÷ adjusted size of space economy (the one they prefer)

–    A rising ratio means each rupee of budget is producing less output, i.e. falling productivity.

# 5. Key Independent (Explanatory) Variables Used

Since this isn't a regression paper, there aren't independent variables in the strict sense. What the paper does have is a set of input and output indicators, and it's worth separating them out because some of them could become variables in our own work.

## Input-side indicators (the "explanatory" side)

•     Space budget — nominal and real, and as % of GDP

•     Human capital — ISRO staff, split into S&T and administrative

•     Tangible space assets — value of satellites and launch vehicles

•     Policy changes — treated qualitatively (1997 Satcom policy, 2011 and 2020 remote sensing policies, 2020 Spacecom draft, geospatial and drone rules of 2021)

## Output / outcome indicators

•     Size of the space economy (the main outcome) and its three segments

•     Space manufacturing output (satellites + launchers)

•     DTH subscribers and revenue; location-service revenue

•     Exports and imports of space products

•     Innovation proxies — publications share, ISRO patent stock and flow, number of start-ups

•     Insurance premiums on satellites launched abroad

•     Capital–output ratio (productivity)

# 6. Main Points of the Paper

•     India's space capability is unusual for a developing country: built almost entirely through indigenous R&D, led by one public organisation, and cheap by global standards ("frugal innovation").

•     Measuring a space economy is genuinely hard everywhere. Different agencies' global estimates differ hugely (STPI's is roughly half of SIA's and Space Foundation's), mostly because of double counting. The authors side with the more conservative STPI view.

•     Space budget intensity has been stuck at around 0.05% of GDP since 2011–12, and the budget actually fell sharply in 2020–21 because of the pandemic. Still, India's space budget is among the larger ones globally.

•     ISRO has shrunk its total workforce but shifted the mix toward scientists and engineers — opposite of the general trend in Indian public science.

•     Manufacturing has been mostly in-house at ISRO, with private firms historically restricted to parts and materials. That is starting to change.

•     Commercially, the satellite side of things in India is basically DTH. Mobile and broadband run on terrestrial networks, so space telecom is narrower than in many other countries.

•     Remote sensing has underperformed commercially — partly restrictive older data policies, partly competition from free Landsat and Sentinel data.

•     India runs a trade deficit in space products, and both imports and exports swing wildly year to year.

•     ISRO hasn't prioritised patenting; a lot of its knowledge is probably tacit because of the strategic, dual-use nature of the technology.

•     A start-up ecosystem is emerging, heavily clustered in Bengaluru, with some in Hyderabad, Chennai and Mumbai, often with ex-ISRO people as advisers.

# 7. Findings

|**What**|**Finding**|
|---|---|
|Size (2020–21)|About USD 5.2 bn unadjusted, USD 4.1 bn adjusted (Rs 38,712 cr / Rs 30,797 cr)|
|Peak|2018–19 at Rs 43,397 cr (≈ USD 6.2 bn); declined in the two years after|
|Share of GDP|Between 0.20% and 0.26% unadjusted; average about 0.23%|
|Structure|Applications ≈ 65–72%, operations ≈ 21–29%, manufacturing ≈ 4–10% — and fairly stable over time|
|Biggest single piece|DTH, about a third of the whole space economy; ~70 million DTH subscribers in 2020|
|Budget intensity|≈ 0.05% of GDP since 2011–12; budget fell ~27% (nominal) in 2020–21|
|Workforce|S&T share of ISRO staff rose from ~69% (2013–14) to ~79% (2020–21)|
|Assets|Grew ~19.5% a year since 2000; went from ~6% to ~14% of comms & broadcasting net capital stock|
|Manufacturing|Peaked around 2015–16; noticeable slowdown since 2017|
|Patents|270+ patents; ~72% in launch vehicles; only 9 at the USPTO; annual grants rose from 2 to 19 (2013–14 → 2019–20)|
|Start-ups|26 listed (Prasad 2021), mostly satellite subsystems, mostly in Bengaluru|
|Global position|About 1.17% of the global space economy; about 2.8% the size of the US space economy|
|Productivity|Capital–output ratio (Est. 2) rose from 0.20 to 0.44 by 2019–20, then dipped to 0.31 in 2020–21|

The productivity result is the one to be careful with. A rising ratio would normally mean falling productivity, but the authors argue a lot of recent spending went into long-gestation, high-risk projects (cryogenic engines, Gaganyaan, semi-cryogenic rockets), so they deliberately don't conclude anything firm from it. Their own estimate for 2019 (≈ USD 6.2 bn) lines up reasonably well with PwC's USD 7 bn.

# 8. Drawbacks / Limitations of the Paper

Some of these the authors admit themselves; others are things we noticed while reading.

## Admitted by the authors

•     Start-ups and offshore development centres are left out because there's no sales data, so the size is underestimated.

•     Intangible assets (tacit know-how, IP) are not valued at all.

•     The capital–output ratio assumes zero time lag between spending and output, which is clearly unrealistic for space technology.

•     Only first-order impacts are covered; the knock-on effects on the rest of the economy are left for future work.

## Our own observations

•     **Several big numbers rest on assumptions.** The 5% rule for location services and the average-cost method for manufacturing are reasonable guesses but aren't justified anywhere. Change the 5% and the applications segment moves a lot.

•     **No causal analysis.** The conclusion that public investment "crowds in" private investment is stated as general belief — the authors admit there's no econometric evidence.

•     **No regional breakdown at all.** Everything is national. You can't tell from this paper which states or cities benefit.

•     **The time window stops at 2020–21,** right when the reforms (IN-SPACe, NSIL, the 2023 policy) start. So it describes the old regime, not the transition.

•     **Mixed price bases.** Real values switch from 2004–05 to 2011–12 prices midway, which makes long-run comparisons a bit shaky.

•     **Patent data is thin.** They aren't sure which jurisdictions ISRO's 270 patents were filed in, and there's no data on whether they were ever used or licensed.

•     **Start-up list comes from one secondary source** and they note Startup India lists 75 — so Table 11 is far from complete.

•     **Small inconsistencies and typos** — e.g. Fig 1 shows India at 0.061% of GDP while Table 3 says 0.05%; a footnote cites "Table 3" when it seems to mean Table 1; the growth rates in Table 13 drop their minus signs. Nothing fatal, but worth double-checking any figure before we quote it.

# 9. How It Will Affect Our Research Paper

This paper is going to be one of our main anchors for the background and the "before" period, so here's how I think it plugs into our spillover project.

•     **It gives us a clean pre-reform baseline.** Their data covers 2011–12 to 2020–21, which lines up almost exactly with our "around 2020" and "before 2020" policy windows. We can cite their figures for what the sector looked like before privatisation instead of building it from scratch.

•     **It confirms the gap we are trying to fill.** They openly say there are no econometric studies on whether public space spending crowds in private activity, and they don't look at regions at all. A regional, before-vs-after design is exactly what's missing — that's a strong line for our synopsis.

•     **It helps us pick the "treated" regions.** Their start-up table points to Bengaluru as the core cluster, with Hyderabad, Chennai, Mumbai and Thiruvananthapuram as secondary ones. That's a starting list for where we'd expect spillovers to show up.

•     **It warns us about using patents as our main outcome.** ISRO historically didn't patent much and kept a lot of knowledge tacit, and 72% of its patents are in launch vehicles. If we go with "patents before vs after privatisation", counts may look low or lumpy for reasons that have nothing to do with the policy. We should probably back it up with a second outcome — new firm formation, ASI manufacturing output in relevant industries, or start-up counts.

•     **It hands us a list of data sources.** Lok Sabha answers, GreyB/PatentsView, TRAI, MoSPI, UN Comtrade and the ESPI report are all things we can reuse. We'll still need sub-national data they didn't use (Indian Patent Office by state, ASI by district/state, maybe Prowess).

•     **The UK Space Agency classification is useful for defining "space-related" industries** when we decide which manufacturing sectors count as high-tech or space-linked.

•     **It points us to the follow-up.** Mani, Dadhwal & Shaijumon's 2025 paper on the space economy's impact on the rest of the economy is the natural next read, since it picks up where this one stops.

•     **A note of caution on numbers:** given the small inconsistencies, any figure we lift from this paper should be cross-checked with the original source (Budget, TRAI, ISRO) before it goes into our draft.

# 1. Main Objective / Research Questions

The policy doesn't ask research questions; it sets out what the government wants. In its own words, the 2020 reforms opened the door for private players, and this document is meant to give them **regulatory certainty** so that a proper commercial space ecosystem can grow.

The objectives, put simply:

•     Let private companies (called NGEs — Non-Government Entities) work across the entire space value chain, not just supply parts.

•     Spell out who does what: IN-SPACe authorises and promotes, ISRO does R&D, NSIL commercialises, DOS oversees.

•     Allow any Indian user of space services to buy from any source, public or private.

•     Push space as a driver of wider technology development, education, start-ups and India's socio-economic goals.

If we turn it into a question from our side: "What exactly changed for private firms in 2023, and which of those changes could plausibly create spillovers into regional manufacturing?"

# 2. Research Design

## On the basis of enquiry

**Not empirical — it's a normative, qualitative document.** There's no data collection or testing. It states what should happen and assigns roles.

## On the basis of objective

**Prescriptive.** Its purpose is to direct behaviour of institutions and firms, not to describe or explain anything.

## On the basis of applicability

**Fully applied / operational.** It's an implementation framework meant to be acted on straight away, with DOS issuing more detailed directives under it.

How it's organised: definitions → vision → strategy → then one section per stakeholder (NGEs, IN-SPACe, ISRO, NSIL, DOS) → applicability. It's essentially a role-allocation map.

# 3. Data Sources (in detail)

There are none. The policy doesn't cite any data, studies, market estimates or consultations. Its basis is the Cabinet-approved reform vision of 2020, and that's it.

What this means for us: the policy can only be used as a **source of dates and rules**, never as evidence of impact. Any claim about what the policy actually did has to come from outside data — which is exactly why it pairs well with the Mani et al. paper. Useful outside sources to match it with would be:

•     IN-SPACe authorisation records and annual updates (number of NGEs authorised, by activity)

•     NSIL annual reports (technology transfers, commercial contracts)

•     Startup India / DPIIT space start-up registrations, ideally by state

•     Indian Patent Office data on space-related filings

•     ASI data for manufacturing activity by state

# 4. Analytical / Statistical Method

Not applicable — there's no analysis in the document. The closest thing to a "method" is the way it splits responsibility among stakeholders and lists permitted activities.

For our purposes, though, the policy suggests a method: it creates a clear before/after break. Reforms announced in 2020, consolidated in this 2023 policy — so it naturally lends itself to an event-study or difference-in-differences setup, comparing regions or industries more exposed to the reforms with those less exposed.

# 5. Key Independent (Explanatory) Variables Used

The document has no variables. But it's basically where our main explanatory variable comes from, so here's what we could draw out of it:

•     **Reform timing (treatment dummy):** 2020 reforms and the 2023 policy — matching our four planned windows (around 2020, after 2020, around 2023, after 2023).

•     **Private participation / authorisation:** number of NGEs authorised by IN-SPACe, by region or activity.

•     **Exposure to space clusters:** IN-SPACe is told to promote clusters, zones, manufacturing hubs and incubation centres — so being in or near one could be a treatment intensity variable.

•     **Technology transfer:** ISRO-to-industry transfers (via IN-SPACe and NSIL) as a spillover channel.

•     **Data openness:** ISRO remote sensing data at GSD ≥ 5 m becomes free and open; finer data available at fair pricing to NGEs. This could matter for downstream application firms.

•     **ISRO's exit from operational manufacturing:** could be modelled as a shift in demand toward private manufacturers.

# 6. Main Points of the Policy

## Private players (NGEs)

•     NGEs include companies, LLPs, trusts and associations registered in India.

•     They can now do end-to-end space activities: build and run satellites, ground stations and control centres, offer communication and remote sensing services, build and launch rockets, run launch pads, do space situational awareness, and even commercially recover asteroid or space resources.

•     They can use Indian or foreign orbital resources and file with the ITU directly through DoT/WPC, or even through foreign administrations.

## IN-SPACe

•     Autonomous body; the single window for authorising space activities by both government and private players.

•     Also has a promotional role: building industry clusters and hubs, linking industry and academia, setting standards, sharing best practices.

•     Must ensure a level playing field for publicly funded facilities — and its prioritisation decisions are binding on the operators.

•     Identifies ISRO technologies ready for transfer and facilitates the transfer.

•     High-resolution remote sensing data (GSD ≤ 30 cm) needs authorisation; coarser than that only needs intimation.

## ISRO

•     Moves back to its core: R&D in new technologies, science, human spaceflight.

•     Will **transition out of manufacturing operational space systems**; mature systems get handed to industry.

•     Shares technologies and best practices with NGEs, and opens up its remote sensing data.

## NSIL and DOS

•     NSIL commercialises technologies built with public money and serves both government and private users on commercial terms.

•     DOS is the nodal department — it oversees the whole thing, interprets ambiguities, handles international coordination, debris mitigation and dispute resolution.

## Scope

•     Applies to all space activity to or from Indian territory, up to the exclusive economic zone. Government keeps the right to grant case-by-case exemptions.

# 7. Findings

A policy has no findings, so I've written these as the key takeaways from reading it critically:

•     The big shift is from ISRO as a builder to ISRO as a researcher, with private industry taking over manufacturing and operations.

•     IN-SPACe is designed as both regulator and promoter — that dual role is central to how the reforms are supposed to work.

•     The policy explicitly backs **regional clustering** (hubs, zones, incubators), which is directly relevant to our cluster-spillover idea.

•     Technology transfer from ISRO is now formalised through IN-SPACe and NSIL — this is the most obvious channel through which spillovers could travel.

•     Demand side is opened up too: any Indian user can buy space services from anyone, which should widen the market for private firms.

•     It reads as a framework, not a finished system — lots of "shall issue guidelines" and "from time to time", meaning a lot of the real detail was left for later.

# 8. Drawbacks / Limitations

•     **No legal backing on its own.** It's a policy, not a law. Things like liability, insurance and dispute resolution are mentioned but left to future guidelines or legislation.

•     **No timelines or targets.** Nothing says by when ISRO exits manufacturing, how many firms should be authorised, or what success looks like. That makes it hard to evaluate.

•     **No budget or incentive figures.** The policy talks about promotion schemes and incentives but commits no money.

•     **Vague wording in key places** — "fair and transparent pricing", "sound commercial principles", "judiciously balance" — leaves a lot to discretion.

•     **Possible conflict of roles.** IN-SPACe sits under DOS, which also controls ISRO and NSIL — so its independence when deciding between ISRO and private competitors could be questioned.

•     **Regulation is spread across ministries.** Broadcasting falls under MIB, telecom under DoT, spectrum under WPC — so firms still deal with multiple regulators despite the "single window" promise.

•     **Investment rules aren't covered here.** Foreign investment in the sector is handled separately, so this document alone doesn't tell the full story of what changed for private capital.

•     **Doesn't cite any evidence** for why these choices were made, and has no built-in review or evaluation mechanism.

# 9. How It Will Affect Our Research Paper

•     **It defines our treatment.** The 2020 reforms and the 2023 policy are the events we're measuring effects around. This document is what we'll cite for exactly what changed and when, so our before/after windows are grounded properly.

•     **It gives a direct theoretical hook for clusters.** The policy itself tells IN-SPACe to build hubs, zones and incubators — so our idea that privatisation affects regional high-tech clusters isn't a stretch; it's something the government explicitly intended. That's a strong motivation line.

•     **It identifies the spillover channels we can test:** technology transfer from ISRO, open data access, private manufacturing replacing ISRO's in-house work, and industry–academia links.

•     **Announcement ≠ implementation.** Since so much of the detail was left for later guidelines, the real "treatment" may have rolled out gradually. We should be careful about using a single cut-off year; a staggered approach or checking for anticipation effects around 2020–2023 might be more honest.

•     **It tells us what to measure, and where data might come from:** NGE authorisations, tech transfers, start-up counts and patents. None of these are in the policy itself, so we'll need to collect them separately.

**Paired with Paper 3,** we get a neat story: Mani et al. show what the sector looked like up to 2020–21 under state control; this policy shows what changed after. Our paper looks at what happened next — specifically in regional manufacturing.
